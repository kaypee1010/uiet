→ Slide 4

Slide 4 · Tortured phrases

Because a machine wrote it, to hide something. pause

These are called tortured phrases. The name comes from three researchers, Guillaume Cabanac, Cyril Labbé and Alexander Magazinov, who in 2021 noticed that some papers were using very strange synonyms for completely standard terms.

Here is what was going on. Someone copied text from an existing paper. Then they pushed it through an automatic paraphrasing tool, a "rewriter", so that plagiarism software would no longer recognise the source. The tool does not understand science. It just swaps words for synonyms. So "breast cancer" becomes "bosom peril". "Artificial intelligence" becomes "counterfeit consciousness". pause And look at the table: "mean square error" becomes "mean square blunder". let them smile

It sounds funny. But look at the numbers. slow By January 2022, their list had 473 of these phrases, in 3,191 peer-reviewed articles. pause More than three thousand papers that went through peer review, and nobody stopped them.

Keep the last two rows in mind: "profound neural organization" and "flag to clamor". They are coming back, for points.

Slide 9 · The pipeline

Every paper travels through the same seven stages: the idea, the data, the analysis, the writing, the figures, the submission, and peer review.

Click Honest lab.

In an honest lab, every one of these stages is done by people, and every step is recorded.

Click Paper mill. Wait for the stages to change.

Now a paper mill. COPE, the Committee on Publication Ethics, defines a paper mill as a profit-oriented, unofficial and potentially illegal organisation that produces and sells fraudulent manuscripts. pause Watch what changes. The data and the analysis are fabricated. The writing is spun, and this is where tortured phrases come from. The figures are recycled from other papers. The author slots are sold: you can literally buy your name onto a paper. And in the worst cases, the mill even supplies fake reviewers, or takes over a whole special issue.

Click Undisclosed AI.

And here is the newer case, which is much closer to home. pause The researcher is real. The data are real. But the writing and the figures are generated by a tool, and nobody is told.

See the difference? The mill is fraud from start to finish. The AI case is one honest person crossing a line in two places.

How big is the mill problem? pause Bigger than most people think.

Slide 12 · Fingerprints

Three real cases. All public, all checkable.

The first one is my favourite. In February 2024, an Elsevier journal called Surfaces and Interfaces published a paper whose introduction began with this sentence: slow "Certainly, here is a possible introduction for your topic." pause That is not the author speaking. That is the chatbot being polite. The authors pasted its reply, greeting and all, and it went through peer review, editing and typesetting without anyone noticing. It was retracted on the 31st of May 2024.

Second. By October 2023, Guillaume Cabanac, the same researcher behind tortured phrases, and his colleagues had flagged more than thirty published papers containing the words "Regenerate response", or "As an AI language model, I…". pause "Regenerate response" is the label on a button in ChatGPT. People copied the button along with the answer.

Third, and this one went viral. In February 2024, a Frontiers journal published a figure of a rat drawn by an AI image tool, with anatomy that was, let's say, biologically impossible, and labels made of nonsense words. It was retracted within days. I won't show you the image; the description is enough. pause

Now, these are the careless ones. The real question is: what about the careful ones? pause Can you tell machine writing from human writing? Let's test the room.

Slide 14 · Detector arithmetic

Imagine a class of one hundred students. This slider is the share who really used a chatbot. I'll start at twenty percent.

Set the slider to 20%. Wait two seconds.

Now let's apply the figures OpenAI published for its own AI detector. It caught 26 percent of AI-written text. And it wrongly flagged 9 percent of human text. Those sound like small errors. pause Watch.

Read the line under the big number. Of 100 students, 20 used AI. The detector catches 5 of them. pause And it wrongly flags 7 who did not. pause So twelve students are flagged, and seven of them did nothing wrong. More than half of the flagged students are innocent.

Slide down to 5%.

And when use is rarer, it gets worse: now most of the flagged students are innocent. This is not a bug you can fix with a better setting. It is arithmetic.

optional a real example that matters for us

There is a second problem, and it matters for us in particular. In 2023, a team at Stanford, Liang and colleagues, published a study in the journal Patterns. They ran essays written by non-native English speakers through several popular AI detectors. pause On average, more than half of those essays, written entirely by humans, were labelled as AI-generated. Simple, careful English, the kind many of us were taught to write, looks "machine-like" to a detector.

OpenAI itself withdrew that detector on the 20th of July 2023, "due to its low rate of accuracy". Turnitin says its detector has under one percent false positives for a whole document, but about four percent at the level of a sentence. And Turnitin itself says the score should start a dialogue, not decide a case. pause

So my advice is simple. A detector score is a reason to talk to someone. pause It is never a verdict.

→ Slide 16

Slide 16 · COPE and ICMJE

In February 2023, COPE, the Committee on Publication Ethics, put it in a position statement. Three points. pause One: AI tools cannot be listed as an author, because they cannot take responsibility for the submitted work. Two: authors must disclose, in the Methods or a similar section, which tool they used, and how. Three: authors are fully responsible for the content of their manuscript, even the parts produced by an AI tool.

The ICMJE, the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors, says the same thing in its own words. Chatbots should not be listed as authors, because they cannot be responsible for the accuracy, integrity and originality of the work. Describe the use in both the cover letter and the paper. And, this is the important line, slow non-disclosure of AI use may be construed as misconduct, in some circumstances. pause

Move your mouse pointer over "responsibility" in the first column, then "responsible" in the second.

Notice the word that appears in both columns. pause Responsible. pause Responsibility is the whole story. And here is why.

→ Slide 17

Slide 17 · Four criteria

The ICMJE says an author must meet all four of these criteria. Not one, not three: all four.

One: a substantial contribution to the idea, the design, the data or the analysis. Two: drafting the work, or revising it critically. Three: final approval of the version to be published. And four: agreeing to be accountable for all parts of the work, being the person who answers any question about it.

Now, a machine can arguably do the first two. It can contribute. It can draft. But can it approve? pause Can it be accountable? pause You cannot email a chatbot a complaint. You cannot ask it to explain its data in front of an ethics committee.

The same four criteria also catch three classic abuses you will meet in your careers. Gift authorship: a name added for status or obligation. The head of department who did nothing. pause Ghost authorship: a real contributor left off the list. A hired writer, a company, or a tool. pause An undisclosed chatbot is a ghost author. And guest authorship: a famous name added to impress the reviewers.

Let's try the rule on a case that many of you will actually face.

→ Slide 20

Slide 20 · Where the line is

The guidance does not draw the line at "did you use AI or not". It draws it at three things: disclosure, accountability, and fabrication.

A, grammar and spelling: fine. Disclose it if a tool rewrote substantial text.

B, summaries for your own notes: fine. But verify before you cite anything from them.

optional a real example

And there is a famous reason for that warning. In 2023, in a New York court case called Mata versus Avianca, two lawyers filed a brief citing six earlier court decisions that ChatGPT had simply invented. pause The cases did not exist. The judge fined the lawyers five thousand dollars. pause A chatbot will give you a reference that looks perfect, and does not exist.

C, writing the discussion: allowed only with disclosure, and you remain responsible for every single claim in it. That is COPE and ICMJE.

D, generating a figure: that is fabrication, unless it is clearly labelled as an illustration. A figure is data. That is the Frontiers rat.

E, generating the data: fabrication. Unit 2.3. pause No disclosure repairs it.

And here is the blind spot. pause Plagiarism software would score all five of these at zero percent similarity. The UGC's 2018 levels, ten, forty and sixty percent, never see any of this.

So who does catch it?

→ Slide 21

Slide 21 · Sleuths and growth

Detection today is a community sport. pause PubPeer: a website where anyone can comment publicly on a published paper. That is where most of today's cases were first flagged. The Problematic Paper Screener, built by Cabanac, Labbé and Magazinov, which scans the published literature for tortured phrases and chatbot text. And Retraction Watch, which keeps the public database of retractions.

Wait for the bars to grow.

Meanwhile, use is rising, and fast. A 2025 study in Nature Human Behaviour looked at 1,121,912 papers, from January 2020 to September 2024. It estimated that by September 2024, up to 22 percent of computer-science abstracts had been modified by a language model. Up to 9 percent in mathematics, and in Nature-portfolio journals. pause

This is not going away. So disclosure has to become the normal thing to do, as normal as citing your statistics package.

→ Slide 23

Slide 23 · Three rules

Three rules you can apply tomorrow, in any discipline.

One. pause Disclose the tool, and what it did. Name it in your Methods and in your cover letter. Silence is the misconduct, not the tool.

Two. pause Never let it make data, figures or references. Generated data is fabrication. And check that every citation it suggests actually exists. pause Remember the lawyers.

Three. pause You sign, you answer. Read every sentence as if a reviewer will ask you about it. pause Because one will.

And none of this is a computer-science problem. The Elsevier paper was about batteries. The Frontiers paper was biology. The Hindawi special issues covered almost everything.
