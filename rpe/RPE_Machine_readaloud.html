→ Slide 4

Tortured phrases

Phrases like this are called tortured phrases. They were first described in 2021 by three researchers: Guillaume Cabanac, Cyril Labbé and Alexander Magazinov.

Here is how they arise. Text is copied from an existing paper and passed through an automatic paraphrasing tool, so that plagiarism software no longer recognises the source. The tool does not understand the subject; it simply replaces words with synonyms. So "breast cancer" becomes "bosom peril", and "artificial intelligence" becomes "counterfeit consciousness". In the table you can see more: "mean square error" becomes "mean square blunder".

By January 2022, the researchers had catalogued 473 such phrases, in 3,191 peer-reviewed articles. All of these papers had passed through peer review.

The last two rows of the table, "profound neural organization" and "flag to clamor", will come back as quiz questions.

→ Slide 6

The paraphrase slider

This slide is a teaching simulation. The phrases are real tortured phrases from Cabanac and colleagues; the percentages are illustrative and are not the output of any real tool.

At the top is an ordinary sentence. Below it are two bars. The first shows what similarity software measures: how much the text overlaps with its source. The second shows how much of the meaning is left for a human reader.

Drag the slider one step at a time, about two seconds per step, to the end.

With each step, one standard term is replaced by its tortured version. The similarity score falls, so a plagiarism report looks cleaner. But the meaning falls as well. At the last step the sentence no longer makes sense, and yet the similarity report looks clean.

Similarity software measures only the first bar: overlapping words. It has no way of knowing whether a sentence still means anything.

Paraphrasing tools themselves are not the problem; many writing tools include a "rephrase" button. The misconduct is using such a tool to hide a copied source.

So who does this on a large scale? Paper mills.

→ Slide 9

The pipeline

Every paper passes through the same seven stages: idea, data, analysis, writing, figures, submission, and peer review.

Click Honest lab.

In an honest lab, every stage is carried out by people, and every step is recorded.

Click Paper mill.

COPE, the Committee on Publication Ethics, defines a paper mill as a profit-oriented, unofficial and potentially illegal organisation that produces and sells fraudulent manuscripts. In a paper mill, the data and the analysis are fabricated. The writing is spun, and this is where tortured phrases come from. Figures are recycled from other papers. Author positions are sold. In the worst cases, the mill also supplies fake reviewers, or takes control of a special issue.

Click Undisclosed AI.

The newer case is different. The researcher is real and the data are real, but the writing and the figures are generated by a tool, and this is not disclosed.

The paper mill is fraud from start to finish. Undisclosed AI use is a genuine researcher crossing a line at particular stages.

→ Slide 10

The 2023 clean-up

Let the numbers finish counting before you speak.

In 2023, retractions across all publishers passed ten thousand for the first time, a record reported by Nature in December 2023.

More than eight thousand of them came from a single publisher, Hindawi, which is owned by Wiley. Its special issues had been taken over by paper mills. Wiley closed four journals as "overrun by paper mills" and reported thirty-five to forty million dollars in lost revenue.

COPE's definition of a paper mill describes them as organisations that "produce and sell fraudulent manuscripts that seem to resemble genuine research". The key words are "seem to resemble": these papers are designed to look genuine.

→ Slide 12

Fingerprints

Here are three real, publicly documented cases.

First: in February 2024, the Elsevier journal Surfaces and Interfaces published a paper whose introduction began with the sentence, "Certainly, here is a possible introduction for your topic." That is a chatbot's reply, pasted in without editing, and it passed through review and publication unnoticed. The paper was retracted on 31 May 2024 for undisclosed use of generative AI.

Second: by October 2023, Guillaume Cabanac and colleagues had flagged more than thirty published papers containing the phrases "Regenerate response" or "As an AI language model". "Regenerate response" is the label of a button in the ChatGPT interface; it was copied into the papers along with the generated text.

Third: in February 2024, a Frontiers journal published a figure of a rat that had been generated by an AI image tool, with incorrect anatomy and meaningless labels. The paper was retracted within days.

These are the careless cases. The more important question is whether careful use can be detected at all. The next question tests that.

→ Slide 14

Detector arithmetic

Consider a class of one hundred students. The slider sets the share who actually used a chatbot. I will start at twenty percent.

Set the slider to 20%.

Now apply the figures that OpenAI published for its own AI detector: it identified 26 percent of AI-written text, and it wrongly flagged 9 percent of human-written text.

The line under the large number gives the result. Of 100 students, 20 used AI. The detector catches 5 of them, and wrongly flags 7 who did not. So twelve students are flagged, and seven of them did nothing wrong. More than half of the flagged students are innocent.

Slide down to 5%.

When actual use is lower, the problem becomes worse: most of the flagged students are innocent. This follows from the arithmetic; it cannot be fixed simply by adjusting a threshold.

optional

There is a second, related problem. In 2023, researchers at Stanford, Liang and colleagues, published a study in the journal Patterns. They tested several popular AI detectors on essays written by non-native speakers of English. On average, more than half of these human-written essays were classified as AI-generated. Plain, careful English can look "machine-like" to a detector.

OpenAI withdrew its detector on 20 July 2023, citing its low rate of accuracy. Turnitin reports under one percent false positives at the level of a whole document, but about four percent at the level of individual sentences, and Turnitin itself advises that its score should start a dialogue, not decide a case.

A detector score can be a reason for a conversation. It should never be treated as a verdict.

Publishers and ethics bodies have since given a clear answer.

→ Slide 16

COPE and ICMJE

In February 2023, COPE, the Committee on Publication Ethics, issued a position statement with three main points. First, AI tools cannot be listed as an author, because they cannot take responsibility for the submitted work. Second, authors must disclose, in the Methods or a similar section, which tool was used and how. Third, authors are fully responsible for the content of their manuscript, including any parts produced by an AI tool.

The ICMJE, the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors, whose authorship rules are widely used beyond medicine, takes the same position. Chatbots should not be listed as authors, because they cannot be responsible for the accuracy, integrity and originality of the work. The use should be described in both the cover letter and the submitted work. And non-disclosure of AI use may be construed as misconduct in some circumstances.

The common idea in both statements is responsibility, and that is what authorship rests on.

→ Slide 17

Four criteria

According to the ICMJE, an author must meet all four of these criteria.

One: a substantial contribution to the idea, design, data or analysis. Two: drafting the work or revising it critically. Three: final approval of the version to be published. Four: agreement to be accountable for all parts of the work, and to answer any question about it.

An AI tool can arguably contribute to the first two. It cannot give final approval, and it cannot be held accountable. That is why it cannot be an author.

The same criteria also identify three well-known authorship abuses. Gift authorship: a name added for status or obligation, without a real contribution. Ghost authorship: a real contributor left off the author list, such as a hired writer, a company, or a tool; an undisclosed chatbot is a form of ghost authorship. Guest authorship: a well-known name added to impress reviewers.

→ Slide 20

Where the line is

The guidance does not draw the line at whether AI was used. It draws it at three things: disclosure, accountability, and fabrication.

A, grammar and spelling: acceptable. Disclose it if a tool rewrote substantial parts of the text.

B, summaries for your own notes: acceptable, but verify anything before you cite it.

optional

There is a well-known example of why this matters. In 2023, in a New York court case, Mata versus Avianca, two lawyers submitted a legal brief that cited six earlier court decisions invented by ChatGPT. The cases did not exist, and the court fined the lawyers five thousand dollars. A chatbot can produce a reference that looks entirely genuine but does not exist.

C, writing the discussion: allowed only with disclosure, and the authors remain responsible for every claim. This is the position of COPE and the ICMJE.

D, generating a figure: fabrication, unless it is clearly labelled as an illustration, because a figure is data. This is the Frontiers case.

E, generating the data: fabrication, Unit 2.3. No disclosure can make it acceptable.

Finally, note the blind spot. Plagiarism software would give all five of these a similarity score of zero percent. The UGC 2018 similarity levels of ten, forty and sixty percent do not detect any of them.

→ Slide 21

Sleuths and growth

Today, detection is largely a community effort. PubPeer is a website where anyone can comment publicly on published papers, and most of the cases in this talk were first flagged there. The Problematic Paper Screener, built by Cabanac, Labbé and Magazinov, scans the published literature for tortured phrases and chatbot text. And Retraction Watch maintains a public database of retractions.

Let the bars finish growing.

At the same time, AI use is increasing rapidly. A 2025 study in Nature Human Behaviour analysed 1,121,912 papers published between January 2020 and September 2024. It estimated that by September 2024, up to 22 percent of computer-science abstracts had been modified by a large language model, and up to 9 percent in mathematics and in Nature-portfolio journals.

This trend will continue, which is why disclosure needs to become standard practice, in the same way that we cite software and statistical packages.

→ Slide 23

Three rules

To conclude, here are three rules that apply in any discipline.

First: disclose the tool and what it did. Name it in the Methods section and in the cover letter. It is the non-disclosure that constitutes misconduct, not the use of the tool.

Second: never let it produce data, figures or references. Generated data is fabrication. And check that every citation it suggests actually exists.

Third: you sign, you answer. Read every sentence as if a reviewer will ask you about it, because one may.

These issues are not limited to any one field. The Elsevier paper was about batteries, the Frontiers paper was in biology, and the Hindawi special issues covered a wide range of subjects.
