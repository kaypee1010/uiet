The set-up that works best online

  • Share one browser tab, not your screen. In Google Meet: Present now → A tab and pick the deck. (Zoom: Share → Advanced → Chrome tab or share the Chrome window; Teams: share the window.) Tab sharing means notifications, this script and other windows are never seen, and you can click between windows freely.
  • The deck, with your PIN already in it. Bookmark and open https://kpsingh.in/uiet/rpe/RPE_Machine_slides.html?pin=YOUR-PIN#s2 (your real PIN; it is deliberately not written on this public page). The page takes the PIN into memory and removes it from the address bar, so it never shows on the shared tab. Keep the bookmark private.
  • Do not press N while sharing. The notes panel is part of the shared tab; this page replaces it.
  • Your audience's phone is their buzzer. Participants scan the QR on slide 2 with their phone, or open kpsingh.in/uiet/join from the chat. Phones are best. On a laptop the quiz sits in a second window: tell them to keep it beside the meeting. The tab title shows ● when a question opens.
  • Latency to expect: the audience sees your shared screen 1–3 seconds late; a question reaches their phones within about 3 seconds of pressing O. So always give at least 30 seconds before C, and say the question aloud.
  • Capacity: the Google Sheet backend is comfortable up to about 60 people answering at once. For a coursework class it is well inside that.

30 minutes before

  1. Laptop on power, notifications off (Windows: Focus / Do not disturb), all other tabs closed. Wired internet or strong Wi-Fi, and your phone's hotspot ready as a backup.
  2. Open the deck bookmark (with the PIN). Slide 2 must show the pill live · connected · PIN set. Press Presenter setup → Reset session to clear any test players, then close that fold.
  3. Press Copy link for chat on slide 2 once, so the join link is on your clipboard.
  4. Rehearse the share: join the meeting from your phone as a guest, present the deck tab from the laptop and check the slides are readable on the phone. Leave the phone's meeting audio muted to avoid echo, then leave the meeting on the phone.
  5. Open this script on your second device. Go to slide 1 in the deck, press F11 for full screen, and start sharing only when the host hands over.

While presenting: five habits

  1. Say every question aloud before pressing O; some people are listening, not watching.
  2. Narrate the numbers: "twelve answers in… fifteen… closing in five." Online silence feels twice as long as in a room.
  3. Paste the link twice: on slide 2 and again on slide 3 for latecomers.
  4. Use names from the chat and the leaderboard ("Priya is leading, 137 points"): it is the online equivalent of eye contact.
  5. Discussion prompts go to the chat ("type one word: what gave it away?"), then read two answers aloud. Invite one person to unmute only if the class usually does.

Slide 3 · Q1 "bosom peril" (scored) 2 min · 4

Chat: paste the join link again for latecomers.

"Question one. A published paper studies patients with 'bosom peril'. What did the authors mean? A, a chest infection; B, heart disease; C, breast cancer; D, anxiety. It's open on your phones now; you have thirty seconds, and speed earns points."

Do: press O as you finish reading. Narrate the count shown on the slide ("twelve answered… eighteen…"). At about 30 seconds: "Five seconds." Press C.

Transition: "Because a machine wrote it, to hide something."

Slide 4 · Tortured phrases 1.5 min · 5.5

"These are called tortured phrases. In 2021 three researchers, Guillaume Cabanac, Cyril Labbé and Alexander Magazinov, noticed that some papers used bizarre synonyms for standard terms. The cause: copied text had been pushed through an automatic paraphrasing tool so that plagiarism software would no longer recognise the source. The tool swaps 'artificial intelligence' for 'counterfeit consciousness' because a thesaurus does not know that the phrase is a fixed technical term."

"By January 2022 their list had 473 distinct tortured phrases, in 3,191 peer-reviewed articles. Look at the table: 'mean square blunder' for mean square error, 'profound neural organization' for deep neural network, 'flag to clamor' for signal to noise. Keep those last two in mind; they come back for points."

Do: let the counters finish before you read the numbers.

Transition: "Where does this sit in our syllabus?"

Slide 5 · Syllabus map 1 min · 6.5

"Everything today is anchored to our RS-9003 syllabus. Fabrication, point 2.3 in Unit 2: machine-made data, figures and references. From Unit 3, the types of publication misconduct and the problems that lead to it, 3.4, and how misconduct is identified, 3.6. From Unit 5, the software tools: what Turnitin, Urkund and AI detectors can and cannot see. And near the end, authorship, 3.5, because that is where the question 'can an AI be an author' lives."

Transition: "Let me show you the trick working."

Slide 6 · Playground: paraphrase slider 2.5 min · 9

"This is a simulation I built for teaching. The phrases are real tortured phrases from Cabanac and colleagues; the percentages are illustrative, not the output of any tool. Here is a normal sentence about a deep neural network used for breast-cancer screening."

Do: drag the slider one step at a time. Pause after each step so the shared screen catches up (1–2 seconds).

"One step: 'artificial intelligence' becomes 'counterfeit consciousness'. Would Turnitin flag this? The similarity score has dropped. Next step… 'mean square blunder'. Similarity falls again, and so does the meaning. By the last step the sentence is nonsense, and the plagiarism report looks clean."

Chat: "Quick one in the chat: which of these two bars does Turnitin actually measure, the top or the bottom?" Read two answers.

"Only the first. Similarity software counts overlapping words. It has no idea what the sentence means."

Transition: "Two more phrases to decode, for points."

Slide 8 · Q3 "flag to clamor" (scored) 1.5 min · 12

"Question three. A 'flag to clamor' ratio is a tortured version of… A, a protest signal; B, a noise complaint; C, a warning sign; D, signal to noise. Open now."

Do: O, 25 seconds, C, R.

"Signal to noise. 'Signal' became 'flag', 'noise' became 'clamor'. If you've ever pressed a 'rephrase' button in a writing tool, you've used the same engine. The tool is not the offence; hiding a copied source with it is."

Do: glance at the leaderboard strip or rail and name the leader: "Right now Priya is leading."

Transition: "So who does this at scale? Paper mills."

Slide 9 · Playground: the pipeline 2 min · 14

"Every paper travels the same seven stages: idea, data, analysis, writing, figures, submission, peer review."

Do: click Honest lab.

"In an honest lab, every stage is done by people and recorded."

Do: click Paper mill; wait for the stages to turn.

"A paper mill sells the whole pipeline. COPE defines a paper mill as a profit-oriented, unofficial and potentially illegal organisation that produces and sells fraudulent manuscripts. The data and analysis are fabricated, the writing is spun, and this is where tortured phrases come from. Figures are recycled, author slots are sold, and in the worst cases the mill supplies fake reviewer accounts or takes over a special issue."

Do: click Undisclosed AI.

"Now the newer case. The researcher is real and the data are real, but the writing and figures are generated, and nobody is told. The mill is fraud from start to finish; the AI case is one honest person crossing a line in two places."

Transition: "How big is the mill problem? Bigger than most people think."

Slide 10 · Paper-mill numbers 1.5 min · 15.5

Do: let the counters run before speaking.

"In 2023 the number of retractions across all publishers passed ten thousand for the first time, a record reported by Nature in December 2023. More than eight thousand of them came from one publisher, Hindawi, owned by Wiley, whose special issues had been taken over by paper mills. Wiley closed four journals as 'overrun by paper mills' and reported thirty-five to forty million dollars of lost revenue."

(Slowly.) "COPE's definition: '…profit oriented, unofficial and potentially illegal organisations that produce and sell fraudulent manuscripts that seem to resemble genuine research.'"

Transition: "Could you spot a mill paper? Question four."

Slide 11 · Q4 spot the mill (scored) 1.5 min · 17

"Which is the strongest sign that a paper came from a mill? A, it has six authors; B, a phrase like 'flag to clamor' where a standard term belongs; C, it is open access; D, it has a very long reference list. Open now."

Do: O, 25 seconds, C, R.

"B, the tortured phrase. Six authors, open access and long reference lists are all perfectly normal. A human expert never writes 'flag to clamor'. That is the fingerprint, and it's what the Problematic Paper Screener searches for."

Transition: "From 2023 the machine got much better at writing, and it started leaving new fingerprints."

Slide 12 · AI fingerprints 1.5 min · 18.5

"Three real, public cases. One: a paper in Elsevier's journal Surfaces and Interfaces, published in February 2024, whose introduction began with the chatbot's own reply: 'Certainly, here is a possible introduction for your topic.' It was retracted on 31 May 2024 for undisclosed generative-AI use."

"Two: by October 2023, Guillaume Cabanac and colleagues had flagged on PubPeer more than thirty published papers containing 'Regenerate response' or 'As an AI language model'. The authors had copied the button text along with the answer."

"Three: in February 2024 a Frontiers journal published an AI-drawn figure of a rat with nonsense labels. It was retracted within days, after the figures were shown to be AI-generated. I won't show the image; the description is enough."

Transition: "Can you tell machine writing from human writing? Let's test the room."

Slide 14 · Playground: detector arithmetic 2.5 min · 23

"Imagine a class of one hundred. This slider is the share who really used a chatbot. I'll start at twenty percent."

Do: set the slider to 20%. Pause two seconds for the share to catch up.

"Now apply the figures OpenAI published for its own detector: it caught 26 percent of AI text and wrongly flagged 9 percent of human text. Read the sentence under the big number: of 100 students, 20 used AI. The detector catches 5 of them and wrongly flags 7 who did not. So of the twelve students flagged, more than half did nothing wrong."

Do: slide down to 5%.

"When use is rarer, it gets worse: most of the flagged students are innocent. That's base-rate arithmetic, not a flaw you can fix with a better threshold."

Slide 16 · COPE and ICMJE 1.5 min · 25.5

"COPE, the Committee on Publication Ethics, in its position statement of 13 February 2023: AI tools cannot be listed as an author, because they cannot take responsibility for the submitted work. Authors must disclose, in the Methods or a similar section, which tool they used and how. And authors are fully responsible for the content of their manuscript, even the parts produced by an AI tool."

"The ICMJE, the medical journal editors' body, says the same: chatbots should not be listed as authors because they cannot be responsible for the accuracy, integrity and originality of the work. Describe the use in both the cover letter and the submitted work. And, importantly, non-disclosure of AI use may be construed as misconduct in some circumstances."

Do: move your mouse pointer over the word "responsibility" in the first column, then "responsible" in the second: online, the pointer is your finger.

Transition: "Responsibility is the whole story, and here is why: the authorship rules."

Slide 17 · Four authorship criteria 1.5 min · 27

"The ICMJE says an author must meet all four criteria: a substantial contribution to the idea, design, data or analysis; drafting or critically revising the work; final approval of the version to be published; and agreement to be accountable for all parts of the work. A machine can arguably do the first two. It cannot approve, and it cannot be accountable: you cannot email it a complaint."

"The same test catches the three classic abuses. Gift authorship: a name added for status or obligation, a boss who did nothing. Ghost authorship: a real contributor left off the list, a writer, a company, or a tool; an undisclosed chatbot is a ghost author. Guest authorship: a well-known name added to impress reviewers."

Transition: "Try the rule on a case you will meet."

Slide 18 · Q7 who is an author (scored) 2 min · 29

"Question seven, scored. Your supervisor's friend funded the equipment but never read the paper. Under ICMJE he is… A, first author; B, corresponding author; C, not an author, thank him in the acknowledgements; D, an author, as long as he approves the final draft. Open now, thirty seconds."

Do: O, 30 seconds, C, R.

"C: not an author, acknowledge him. Funding equipment is not one of the four criteria, and approving a draft he never read doesn't make him accountable for it. In practice, agree authorship in writing with your supervisor before the work starts, not after the paper is written."

Transition: "Now the hardest question of the day: where is the line?"

Slide 19 · Q8 draw the line (poll) 2 min · 31

"Five ways people use a chatbot. Pick the first one you would call misconduct. A, fixing grammar and spelling; B, summarising papers for your own notes; C, writing the discussion section; D, generating a figure; E, generating the data. There's no single right answer for the poll: I want to see where this group disagrees. Forty seconds."

Do: O, 40 seconds, C, R.

"The split is usually between C and D." Chat: "If you chose C, one line in the chat: why? If you chose D, why?" Read one from each side.

Transition: "Here is where the guidance draws it."

Slide 20 · Where the line is 1.5 min · 32.5

"The guidance doesn't draw the line at 'used AI or not'. It draws it at three things: disclosure, accountability and fabrication. A, grammar and spelling: fine; disclose if a tool rewrote substantial text. B, summaries for your own notes: fine; verify before you cite anything from them. C, writing the discussion: allowed only with disclosure, and you remain responsible for every claim; that's COPE and ICMJE. D, generating a figure: fabrication unless it's clearly labelled as an illustration, because a figure is data; that's the Frontiers case. E, generating the data: fabrication, Unit 2.3; no disclosure repairs it."

"And the blind spot: plagiarism software would score all five at zero percent similarity. The UGC 2018 levels of ten, forty and sixty percent never see any of this."

Transition: "So who catches it?"

Slide 23 · Three rules 1 min · 36.5

"Three rules you can apply tomorrow, in any discipline. One: disclose the tool and what it did. Name it in the Methods and the cover letter. Silence is the misconduct, not the tool. Two: never let it make data, figures or references. Generated data is fabrication, and check that every citation it suggests actually exists. Three: you sign, you answer. Read every sentence as if a reviewer will ask you about it, because one will."

Likely questions

Is Grammarly or a spell-checker "AI use" that must be disclosed?

COPE and ICMJE target substantive use: text generation, rewriting, summarising, analysis. Basic grammar and spelling correction does not normally need disclosure. If a tool rewrote whole paragraphs, disclose it.

Can I use a chatbot to translate my paper into English?

Most publishers treat that as a disclosable writing aid: say so in Methods or the cover letter, and you remain responsible for the accuracy of every sentence in the translation.

Can I use it for the literature review?

To search, sort and summarise for yourself, yes, with two rules: verify every claim and every citation against the original source (these tools invent references), and never paste its summary into your text as if you wrote it, without disclosure.

Does UGC have a rule on AI-generated text?

The UGC 2018 plagiarism regulations define similarity levels; they say nothing about generated text, which scores zero similarity. Individual universities and funders have started issuing AI policies; check yours and your target journal's. Do not claim a national rule that does not exist.

Where do the 26% and 9% figures come from?

From OpenAI's own page for its AI text classifier, January 2023: it identified 26 percent of AI-written text as "likely AI-written" and mislabelled 9 percent of human text. The page carries the note that the classifier was withdrawn on 20 July 2023 for low accuracy. The simulator applies those two rates to a class of 100; it is arithmetic, not a study of any real class.

Are the retraction figures certain?

The "more than 10,000 in 2023" figure is from Nature's news analysis of 12 December 2023, based on Retraction Watch data; "more than 8,000" from Hindawi is Wiley's own figure reported by Retraction Watch on 19 December 2023. Both are counts at that date and have since grown.

What is the Problematic Paper Screener?

A public tool built by Cabanac, Labbé and Magazinov that scans the published literature for signals such as tortured phrases and chatbot text, and lists flagged papers for human checking. It flags; people judge.

How do I disclose AI use in practice?

One or two sentences in Methods or Acknowledgements: which tool, which version, what it was used for (for example, "language editing of the Introduction"), and a statement that the authors reviewed and take responsibility for the content. Repeat it in the cover letter if the journal follows ICMJE.
